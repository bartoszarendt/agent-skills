# Landing pages and portfolios

Use these considerations for marketing and editorial surfaces. Choose structure
from the audience, message, and available content, while preserving the brief.

## Inventory the content before choosing structure

Identify what the page can honestly show: supplied or verified facts, prices,
dates, product behavior, writing, photographs, and authorized customer material.
Distinguish usable assets from temporary imagery, illustrative samples, and proof
that is absent. Keep the source or status clear where someone could mistake a
sample for a fact.

Let this inventory constrain the composition. A customer quote can lead when a
real, attributable quote is available. A product tour needs legible captures or
a working demonstration of the relevant behavior. A measured result needs its
unit, context, and supporting evidence. When those materials are missing, choose
a structure the available content can carry rather than inventing proof or
filling repeated empty frames.

A clearly identified mockup can be useful when the request is for a concept.
Do not present it as a capture of an existing product. Do not place a sample
testimonial or logo where it implies a real endorsement or relationship. Missing
practical facts, such as opening hours or a price, need an explicit placeholder
or an omission made clear in the handoff; plausible values are not substitutes.

## Main message and action

Make the product or subject identifiable and the primary action easy to find.
Fit the most useful content into the initial view where practical, without
forcing copy or typography into a fixed line count.

Use imagery when it explains the subject, demonstrates the product, or establishes
the intended identity. A text-led page can be complete when the message carries
the design. Avoid adding a decorative visual solely to satisfy a template.

Keep navigation understandable and usable at supported widths. Shorten labels
only when their meaning survives. Do not silently remove useful routes to fit
a preferred layout.

## Build around the visitor's job

Identify what the visitor came to understand or do, the information they need
before acting, and what the page should make possible. Place the action where
that readiness occurs. It may belong in the opening view, beside an offer or
availability, or after supporting material. Repeat it where the reading path
justifies it, using a consistent name and destination.

Use the examples below to test the page's anatomy, not to assign a fixed layout
from a category. A mixed-purpose page may need more than one path; prioritize
them from the brief and actual audience.

| Visitor job | Content that may carry the page | Action and closing considerations |
|---|---|---|
| Subscribe or follow | A representative issue, episode, archive, or explanation of what arrives | Put the subscription near the sample or opening promise; state the relevant cadence and commitment |
| Book | The service, menu, room, class, availability, and practical details | Keep booking near the offer or available times; make location, hours, and conditions easy to find |
| Visit | What is available now, where it is, and how to get there | Give relevant hours, address, access, and ticket information a clear place; close with useful visit details |
| Attend | The actual dates, place, program, and ticket choices | Put ticket or registration access near the decision information; retain travel and attendance details |
| Buy | The product, options, price, use, delivery, and guarantee | Keep the chosen option and its terms close to buying; end on the offer or practical reassurance when useful |
| Try, download, or sign up | The real product at work, supported platforms, setup, and applicable terms | Offer the route the product actually supports; close on trying it, installation, or the relevant next step |
| Hire or enquire | Selected work, scope of service, and a way to contact the provider | Put contact access near the work and after it; use a form only when it serves the enquiry |
| Join | What participation involves, where and when it happens, and any requirements | Make the first visit or joining path clear; put relevant membership choices and practical details beside it |
| Read, listen, or browse | The writing, release, episode, or collection itself | Expose the content and useful continuation; do not interrupt it with an unrelated conversion flow |

Choose an ending that completes the page's job: the offer again, contact or visit
details, a next issue, related work, or a useful continuation. An email form is
appropriate when following future material is part of the visitor's purpose;
do not append one by default to every page.

## Structure and density

Group information by the reader's questions and decisions. Vary layouts when the
content needs a different relationship; repeat them when consistency aids scanning.

Use lists for comparable items, tables for comparisons, cards for independent
units, and sequence markers for actual sequences. Do not replace a clear list
with a carousel merely because it contains more than a fixed number of items.

Keep spacing, headings, and emphasis consistent with hierarchy. Choose density
based on the amount of useful information and supported viewport. Define a
responsive treatment for every multi-column composition.

## Content and proof

Use precise claims supported by supplied or verified information. Label sample
metrics, testimonials, logos, and product mockups. Do not imply customer
relationships or endorsements without evidence.

Prefer descriptive CTA labels and consistent names for the same action. Keep
supporting copy concise without dropping information needed to decide.

Place proof near the claim it supports. A logo row, quote, demonstration, or
comparison should serve a purpose; none is mandatory.

Make the interaction match the promise. A booking link must reach the intended
booking route; a download must identify what it provides; a subscription must
distinguish pending, failed, and confirmed submission. Do not show a success
message merely because a button was pressed. If the authorized deliverable is a
static concept or integration is unavailable, identify that boundary clearly
instead of implying that the visitor completed the task.

## Assets and motion

Use imagery with the right subject, crop, resolution, and rights for the task.
Preserve required attribution. Ensure text and marks remain legible in each
supported scheme.

Use motion only when it contributes to understanding or feedback. Avoid
interactions that obscure content, interfere with navigation, or disregard
reduced-motion preferences.

## Verification

- [ ] The subject, value, and primary action are clear.
- [ ] Structure and the ending support the visitor's job and available material without arbitrary quotas or hidden information.
- [ ] Claims and proof are accurate; samples and temporary assets are identified.
- [ ] Navigation and actions work at supported widths and input modes.
- [ ] Outcomes reflect real behavior; mockups and incomplete integrations are explicit.
- [ ] Layout, contrast, and motion meet the applicable accessibility requirements.
