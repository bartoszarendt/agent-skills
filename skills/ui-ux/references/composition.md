# Composition and hierarchy

Use this reference when arranging a page, application screen, or substantial
component. Choose composition from the relationships in the content and the
work people need to do. A color or spacing correction may need no new layout.

## Establish the relationships

Identify what the reader needs to notice first, what supports it, and what they
must compare or act on together. Inspect the actual content and supported states
before choosing a structural family. A layout built around a photograph, measured
result, or product demonstration needs that material, or an honestly identified
sample appropriate to the requested deliverable.

Separate the information structure from its visual treatment. The same schedule
can use quiet rules or expressive typography while retaining its dates, order,
and actions. A new palette does not justify moving controls, replacing a table,
or changing navigation. Preserve the established flow unless its redesign is in
scope.

For a work surface, identify the object people repeatedly use: a queue, ledger,
editor, timeline, or search result. Give that object usable space and keep its
controls nearby. For an editorial or promotional page, identify what carries the
message: the writing, collection, place, product, or supplied proof. Do not give
every kind of screen a large introductory hero.

## Choose a structural family

Use these as starting points, not templates or a required menu. Combine them
when the content has different relationships; keep a simpler arrangement when
it serves the task.

| Content relationship | Useful starting point | Failure to check | Constrained treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Follow an explanation or argument | A continuous reading column with relevant figures and notes | Breaking connected prose into unrelated tiles | Keep the reading sequence; place a note near its reference |
| Find a record or compare exact values | Aligned rows or a table with stable identifiers | Cards that force people to remember values across positions | Keep essential comparisons together; use deliberate scrolling or supported detail views |
| Browse independent items | A list or grid with comparable titles, images, and metadata | Uniform visual weight hiding the distinction people browse by | Reduce columns or use rows while preserving identity and useful metadata |
| Understand a claim with its evidence | A shared figure or a split of explanation and proof | A large visual that neither supports the claim nor remains legible | Stack the explanation and evidence in a meaningful order |
| Follow a real sequence | Stages with their inputs, outputs, or moments | Numbered feature tiles that imply an order the product does not have | Keep the actual sequence in normal reading order |
| Understand relationships in space | A labeled map, diagram, or spatial overview | Positions or decoration that imply unsupported relationships | Provide usable navigation and equivalent information when the diagram cannot fit |
| Work in a selected item | A master list with detail, or a focused editor with its controls | A narrow work area dominated by summaries or navigation | Preserve selection and context when switching between list and detail |
| Make a choice or complete a transaction | The offer or fields beside the information needed to decide | Supporting material separating an option from its price, terms, or action | Keep dependent information together and preserve the established completion flow |

For example, an operations queue may need aligned status, owner, age, and action
columns rather than a row of summary cards. A publication may need a readable
article and a quiet contents list. A studio portfolio may lead with the work and
its project labels. Use these relationships only when the actual content and
task support them; an industry name does not select the layout.

## Establish hierarchy and visual weight

Choose the first point of attention deliberately. Use scale, placement, contrast,
spacing, and imagery together, then check what wins in the rendered result. A
bright decoration or oversized heading can displace the record, product, or
action the screen exists for.

Give the lead element enough space to be understood. Crop imagery around what
matters; show a product at a size where its relevant behavior is legible. A
large empty frame has visual weight without adding information. A text-led page
can use type and a strong reading structure without inventing an illustration.

Make supporting levels distinct without making each one compete for attention.
Keep labels close to their values and actions close to the objects they affect.
Align information that people compare, such as prices, dates, column headings,
and row actions. Use the same unit and precision for comparable values where
the domain permits it.

Choose alignment from the content. A centered statement can work as a single
focal object; a dense table usually needs stable row and column edges. Check
whether a heading belongs to the whole region or a particular column. Avoid
accidental offsets caused by unrelated container widths or padding.

## Group, pace, and repeat

Use proximity, alignment, rules, surface changes, or containers to make groups
recognizable. Add a card boundary when it expresses an independent item or
interaction, not around every heading and paragraph. Keep nested surfaces only
when the containment has meaning and the hierarchy remains readable.

Repeat composition for comparable content so people can scan it. Change
composition when a different relationship needs it: a product example may be
followed by an aligned comparison and then practical details. Do not alternate
sides, shuffle sections, or vary column counts merely to avoid repetition.

Carry the chosen visual system into the whole surface. Apply its type roles,
spacing relationships, rule weights, surfaces, and control conventions to lower
sections and consequential states. A distinctive first view followed by an
unrelated component system is an incomplete direction.

Set density from the task, content volume, and viewing conditions. A frequent
work surface can keep rows compact while preserving readable values and usable
targets. A long article can give prose a comfortable measure while allowing a
figure to extend beyond it. Do not force every region into the same width or
make every section fill a screen.

## Adapt without changing meaning

Define how columns, side notes, sticky regions, and dense comparisons behave
when space or input changes. Preserve essential content and actions; do not
hide useful routes or values merely to retain the wide-screen silhouette.

Keep DOM and visual reading orders meaningful. CSS placement must not make
keyboard focus jump through an apparently different sequence. When content
moves from beside to above or below another region, check that explanations,
labels, and actions still refer to the right object. Do not use visual reordering
to repair an incorrect underlying sequence.

Let text growth and real data test the composition. Check long names, wrapped
headings, large values, empty regions, and relevant loading or failure states.
Use containment and wrapping appropriate to the content; clipping an overflow
does not resolve an unreadable column or inaccessible control.

## Verification

- [ ] The lead object and hierarchy support the reader's task.
- [ ] Layout expresses actual comparisons, groups, sequences, or relationships.
- [ ] Repetition supports scanning; variation has a content reason.
- [ ] Alignment, visual weight, and spacing were checked with realistic content.
- [ ] Narrow layouts preserve meaning, reading order, context, and useful actions.
- [ ] Existing behavior and navigation remain within the requested scope.
